    Erosion

    From EROSION.EXE, the free archive of things wearing away

    This article is about wearing away. For the program, see Erosion.exe.

    THIS ARTICLE IS WEARING THIN

    Parts of this page may be missing by the time you reach them. Nothing is deleted. It is only moved, or covered, or forgotten for a while.

    Erosion

    Four lines of the same sentence, each one copied from the one above.

    TypeProcess
    Acts onStone, text, memory, files
    AgentsWater, wind, ice, use, time, others
    SpeedSlow, then all at once
    LeavesScars, sand, gaps

    Erosion is the slow removal of material from a surface. Something wears off, is carried away and settles somewhere else. Rivers do it to mountains, feet do it to stairs, and reading does it to a page. In Erosion.exe the word is used for everything that wears away by being used, copied, opened or left alone: stone, text, memory and files.

    Erosion is not the same as destruction. Nothing that erodes disappears. It is only moved to a place where nobody is looking for it. A valley is what is left after a mountain was carried to the sea one grain at a time, and the sea floor is where the mountain went.

    In stone

    Geologists separate two things. Weathering breaks rock apart where it stands. Erosion is what carries the pieces off: water, wind, ice and gravity. A canyon is erosion that has been going on for a few million years. A stone step in an old church, dipped in the middle, is erosion that took a few hundred years and a great many feet.

    Touch is an agent too. The bronze foot of the statue of Saint Peter in Rome has been kissed and rubbed by pilgrims for centuries, and the toes have worn almost flat. Nobody meant to remove them. Everybody did a little.

    In text

    Text erodes when it is read, copied, translated, summarised or quoted out of order. Each step loses something small and adds something smaller. A sentence that is passed on long enough ends up meaning roughly what the people passing it wanted it to mean.

    Before paper was cheap, writing was scraped off parchment so the parchment could be used again. A page written over like this is called a palimpsest. The old text is never fully gone. With the right light it shows through the new one, and some of the oldest copies of lost works have been found underneath prayer books.

    Scars

    A word that has worn away often leaves a mark where it was: a gap, a smudge, a row of dots. The mark is the only proof that something was there. Scars are more common on pages that nobody reads, which is strange, because nobody is there to wear them down.[citation needed]

    Moved words

    Not every lost word is gone. Some turn up somewhere else on the page, in a sentence that did not ask for them. Over a long session a paragraph can end up saying something its author never wrote. Nothing is added, only moved.

    In memory

    A memory is not stored once and kept. It is rebuilt every time it is remembered, and each rebuild is a copy of the last one, not of the event. Memories that are recalled often stay bright but drift. Memories that are never recalled stay accurate and fade. There is no third option.

    What is left after enough years is usually a feeling, a colour, a smell and one detail that may not have happened.[1]

    In files

    Digital files are said not to wear out. This is mostly true and slightly false. Storage decays, a bit flips, a format stops being readable, a program that opened something is no longer made. The file is still there. It just cannot be reached any more, which from the outside looks the same.

    Copies of copies

    Every time an image is saved as a compressed file, a little detail is thrown away to make it smaller. Saved again, it loses a little more. After a few hundred generations the picture is a field of blocks in roughly the right colours. Tape copied from tape does the same with hiss. In 1969 the composer Alvin Lucier recorded himself speaking in a room, played the recording back into the room and recorded it again, over and over, until his words were gone and only the sound of the room was left.

    Dead links

    A link is a promise that something is still on the other side. Over time the promises break. The page moves, the site closes, the address is sold. A dead link still looks like a door. It just opens onto a wall, or onto something else.[2]

    Agents

    The known agents are water, wind, ice, heat, gravity, use and time. Most erosion can be traced back to one of them.

    Some cannot. Words go missing from pages that have not been opened. Small marks turn up next to the gaps, in short regular rows, as if something had walked there. The marks do not match any known agent, and they are not always in the same place when the page is opened again. Most sources treat this as a fault in the record. A few do not.[citation needed]

    Restoration

    Restoration does not undo erosion. It adds a new layer on top, made to look like the old one, and the new layer starts to wear too. A restored page is a copy standing where the original stood. It is usually good enough.

    On this page a purge restores every word that has gone missing. It is not a cure. The words will go missing again.
